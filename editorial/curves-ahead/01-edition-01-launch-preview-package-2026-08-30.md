**CURVES AHEAD** · Edition 01 · The Breakdown / Ahead of the Curve
*Emerging ideas in business, marketing, technology and media.*
**By Cassandra Sklarz**

---

There has never been a cheaper time to make something look finished.

A business can now produce a presentation, a campaign concept, a research
summary, a video, a landing page or a month's worth of social posts in a
fraction of the time those things once took. That is usually told as a
productivity story.

The more interesting story is what it does to bad decisions. Unclear thinking
can now be distributed at extraordinary speed. A positioning problem can become
thirty posts. A weak idea can become a campaign. A confused strategy can look
extremely polished.

So the question I keep coming back to is this: when execution becomes abundant,
deciding what deserves to be executed becomes the harder problem.

## We spent years treating execution as the bottleneck

A good idea used to meet friction almost immediately. Someone had to research
it, write it, design it, edit it, then resize, format, schedule, distribute and
measure it. Even simple marketing work consumed real time and money, and that
friction limited output.

Much of it is now disappearing. The same team can explore more concepts, produce
more variations, and move from idea to execution far faster. That is genuinely
valuable.

But removing a bottleneck does not guarantee that what flows through it is good.
Sometimes it just exposes the next one. And increasingly, I think the next one is
judgment.

## More output is not the same as more value

Consider a business struggling with content. The obvious response is now easy:
produce more posts, generate more graphics, make more videos, repurpose
everything, automate distribution. The machinery does all of that remarkably
well.

But suppose the real problem is that the company has never answered why anyone
should choose it, who it is actually for, what it understands that its
competitors don't, or what it wants people to associate with its name.

AI does not make those questions disappear. It makes avoiding them more
expensive.

I've become skeptical of adding tools simply because they can perform a task.
The better question is what job the tool is replacing or improving, and when
that isn't clear, the technology tends to create another workflow rather than
simplify one. There is a version of this happening across businesses right now,
where the question being asked is where AI can be added rather than where the
work is actually breaking down. Sometimes AI is the answer. Sometimes the process
should be redesigned first. Sometimes the task should not exist.

The ability to automate something is not evidence that it should exist.

I set up an automated tracker for Curves Ahead because I wanted the publishing
work and progress centralized. Instead of replacing a clear workflow, it became
another system to monitor while higher-priority client work needed attention. I
turned it off until it had a defined job and a real workflow to replace.

## The scarce resource is moving upstream

When production was expensive, production capability created advantage. When
information was hard to reach, access did. When distribution was limited,
distribution did. As each of those became broadly available, advantage moved
somewhere else.

It is moving again, toward a set of questions that are harder to hand off: what
deserves attention, which information is credible, which opportunity and which
audience matter, which signal is meaningful, what to ignore, and what should
happen next.

Those are judgment questions. They are also strategy questions. And they get
more important, not less, when the cost of acting on an answer approaches zero.

## It changes what expertise looks like

Expertise has usually been demonstrated through production. The strategist
produced the deck, the researcher the report, the writer the article, the
designer the system. Those artifacts made expertise visible.

If capable tools can help produce the artifact, the artifact becomes weaker
evidence. The value moves to the thinking behind it: why this recommendation
and not the other ten, why this positioning, this evidence, this audience, and
why now.

Good work will increasingly have to show the choices behind it, not only the
output.

## Content is the obvious case

The internet is about to contain an extraordinary amount of competent content.
Not excellent, not original, not necessarily useful. Competent. Clean graphics,
readable articles, acceptable videos, perfectly serviceable explainers.

When competence becomes abundant, competence stops differentiating anything.

What gets noticed instead is harder to manufacture: a distinctive point of view,
original research, experience, taste, credibility, specificity, unexpected
connections, and a willingness to say that something popular may not actually
matter.

Abundance also creates a verification problem. When producing information becomes
nearly effortless, readers have more to evaluate and fewer reasons to assume that
any given piece deserves their attention. So they fall back on the questions
people always use when they cannot check everything themselves: who produced
this, what do they know, what evidence are they using, do they have a reason to
exaggerate, and have they been useful before.

That makes trust more valuable. I don't think the competitive question ends up
being who adopted AI fastest. Capability is spreading too quickly for access
alone to stay an advantage. The next phase may be less about who can produce the
most and more about who becomes worth listening to.

The objection is partly right. AI will increasingly perform work that looks like
judgment, including ranking, critiquing, and choosing among options. The durable
human advantage is not making every choice manually. It is defining the goal,
deciding which tradeoffs matter, evaluating context the system may not hold, and
remaining accountable for the consequences.

## What I'm watching

Three shifts I'm particularly interested in.

The premium on taste. When almost anyone can generate ten plausible options,
choosing the right one becomes more valuable.

The premium on trust. As synthetic and derivative material gets easier to
produce, identifiable people, credible expertise and evidence become stronger
signals.

And the premium on restraint, which may be the hardest of the three. Technology
tends to make capability visible. Strategy often looks like choosing not to use
all of it.

## The takeaway

AI is reducing the cost of execution, and that matters. But cheaper execution
does not eliminate strategy. It raises the cost of weak strategy, because almost
any decision can now be turned into output before anyone has questioned it.

Execution is getting cheaper.

Judgment isn't.
