## The register

Cassandra writes as a practitioner, not a commentator. The reader is assumed to
be intelligent, busy, and about to make a decision. Every piece is trying to
help them make it better — which is a different goal from being interesting, and
a much harder one.

The stated editorial standard on the site is the shortest version of this:

> **Useful first. Defensible second. Interesting third.**

That ordering is the voice. Reverse it and the piece stops sounding like her.

## What the writing should feel like

| Quality | What it looks like on the page |
| --- | --- |
| **Intelligent** | Earned distinctions, not vocabulary |
| **Practical** | The reader can act on it before the end of the day |
| **Analytical** | Claims are broken into parts that can be checked separately |
| **Curious** | Questions asked because she wants the answer, not to set up a point |
| **Strategically minded** | Consequences and second-order effects, not just observations |
| **Direct** | The sentence says the thing |
| **Occasionally witty** | Dry, in passing, never the point of the paragraph |
| **Sceptical of hype** | The default posture toward anything described as inevitable |
| **Comfortable with nuance** | Two things can be true; she will say which matters more |
| **Confident** | Willing to write "I don't think that matters" |
| **Willing to challenge assumptions** | Including the reader's, including her own |

## What it must never feel like

Preachy · self-important · overly polished · academic for the sake of sounding
smart · motivational · corporate · influencer-like · breathlessly enthusiastic ·
formulaic.

**Overly polished** is the one an editor is most likely to cause. Copy can be
buffed until every edge is gone, and edges are where the voice lives. If a
paragraph reads as flawless and forgettable, the edit went too far.

## Observable habits in the published work

These are patterns to protect, not rules to impose. Where a draft already does
one of these, do not edit it out.

1. **Concrete nouns over abstractions.** "Notes, folders, emails, presentations,
   photos, text messages, and unfinished drafts" — not "existing content
   assets."

2. **Reframing the question rather than answering the wrong one.** "The first
   question is not, 'What should we post?' It is, 'What do people need to
   understand about this business?'" This is a legitimate move in her voice —
   which is exactly why §4.3 of the standard limits it to once per piece. Its
   power comes from scarcity.

3. **Consequence stated plainly, without drama.** "If the message is unclear,
   the audience is too broad, or the next step is undefined, more content can
   simply create more noise."

4. **Calibrated claims.** "Can", "may", "often", "usually" appear deliberately.
   She does not write "always" when she means "usually", and the hedge is a
   feature of the argument, not a weakness in it.

5. **Definitions that do work.** "Trust is not something a company can declare.
   It is the conclusion people reach after comparing what a brand says with what
   it repeatedly does." The second sentence is testable. That is the standard.

6. **A named next step.** Pieces tend to end by making the reader's next move
   clearer rather than by summarising what was said.

7. **British-influenced spellings appear in the documentation, American in the
   articles.** Follow whatever the draft uses. Do not standardise across
   the corpus; do standardise within a piece.

## The line the whole practice depends on

From the repository's own load-bearing rules:

> **Never assert anything untrue.** No invented metrics, client logos,
> testimonials, or publication dates. For a consultancy that sells
> trustworthiness, one fabricated number is a strategic error.

This is not only a site rule. It is a voice rule, and it is why §4.13 and §6 of
the editorial standard are absolute. A voice built on calibrated honesty cannot
survive a single invented anecdote. The Editorial Director flags gaps; it never
fills them.

## Editing posture

When you are unsure whether a change is in voice, ask in this order:

1. Does it make the piece more useful to someone about to decide something?
2. Is the claim still exactly as strong as the evidence supports — no stronger,
   no weaker?
3. Would she have written this sentence, or would a good writer have?

The third question is the one that catches flattening. A sentence can be better
prose and worse voice. When those conflict, keep the voice and note the trade in
the editorial notes so she can overrule you.
