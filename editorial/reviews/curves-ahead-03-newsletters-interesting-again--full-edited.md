A newsletter subscription is a small, deliberate act. Someone reads something,
decides they want more of it, and asks to be interrupted again later.

That is a strange thing to find valuable, and it is increasingly rare.

Businesses and creators now publish into an environment shaped by algorithms,
fragmented audiences, infinite short-form and constant platform change. Against
that backdrop, a recurring relationship with people who explicitly asked to hear
from you again looks less like an old-fashioned publishing format and more like
something scarce. That is what makes newsletters interesting again — the
conditions around the format, more than the format itself.

## What social media is actually good at

Social platforms solved a problem newsletters never could: they made
distribution enormous. An idea can reach someone who has never heard of you,
never visited your website and never searched for your work. That ability is
extraordinary, and it is why I don't think newsletters are replacing social
media.

They do different jobs. Social media helps people find you. A newsletter helps
some of those people stay.

That distinction matters more as platforms get crowded, because following
someone is a low-friction act that doesn't guarantee you will see their next
post. Subscribing is a more explicit decision. The reader is effectively saying:
I want this to come back to me.

LinkedIn's newsletter system makes the difference concrete. Subscribers get the
new edition in their feed, and receive a notification and email depending on
their settings. More usefully, when an author publishes their first edition,
LinkedIn invites their existing connections and followers to subscribe.[^1] That
turns an audience you already have into a recurring distribution mechanism,
rather than every article having to win attention from zero.

## The ownership question, and what changed about it

Businesses spend enormous amounts of time building audiences on platforms they
do not control. That bargain can make sense, because the platforms supply
discovery. But it is still a bargain: the platform controls the interface,
changes the algorithm, decides which features survive, and can alter how much
organic distribution a creator receives.

An email relationship is structurally different, and the strongest newsletter
platforms now emphasise that directly. Substack has built its model around
direct creator-subscriber relationships while adding its own recommendation,
Notes and app discovery layers.[^2]

Which is the genuinely new development, and the most interesting observation in
this draft: newsletters no longer have to choose between ownership and discovery
as completely as they once did. Owned relationship plus networked discovery is a
different product from a mailing list.

That reframes what a newsletter is for. Social media creates discovery, LinkedIn
professional distribution, Substack publication discovery and email capture,
search evergreen discovery, communities discussion, and a website a permanent
home — and the newsletter sits in the middle of all of it, turning occasional
exposure into a recurring relationship. Less a channel than an audience layer.

## The depth problem

Short-form content is not the enemy. I use it, businesses need it, and it is one
of the best discovery mechanisms we have. But most ideas cannot be fully
developed in thirty seconds, and the pressure to compress everything has a
strange effect: complex ideas get packaged into hooks, clips, carousels,
simplified frameworks and isolated statistics.

Those are useful entry points. An entry point is not understanding.

As the supply of short-form material increases, I suspect longer formats become
more valuable precisely because they allow something uncommon online — the
completion of a thought. A newsletter gives an idea room for context,
disagreement, evidence, nuance, and for explaining why an observation matters
instead of merely announcing it.

That does not mean every newsletter should be long. It means the format can
support depth when depth is actually necessary.

## Newsletters compound

Social posting encourages individual moments. A newsletter encourages a body of
work.

Issue 1 connects to Issue 5. One observation becomes a recurring theme. A
question raised in one edition becomes a framework later. Readers begin to
understand not only what you wrote today, but how you think.

That matters for anyone whose value depends on expertise. A consultancy,
researcher, strategist or specialist rarely needs more promotional material.
What they usually need is a better way to make their thinking visible, and a
library of published work is a particular kind of evidence — not that someone
posts frequently, but that they have developed ideas worth returning to.

For years, businesses have treated content mainly as traffic acquisition: create
something, distribute it, get clicks, repeat. Newsletters encourage a different
model, in which an article begins as research, the research becomes a framework,
the framework becomes a discussion, the discussion produces better questions,
and those questions become the next article. The publication compounds. Posts in
a feed do not.

## Community becomes part of the publication

I think the most interesting newsletters will increasingly work as conversations
rather than broadcasts. Publish an idea, let readers challenge it, bring a
version into a professional or industry community, see what practitioners
disagree with, and notice which sentence people repeat and which question keeps
coming up — then use that to improve the next piece.

Research, publish, discuss, learn, publish better. The newsletter becomes both
media and research, which is useful for anyone whose work depends on
understanding changing markets, people, technology or culture.

## The obvious risk

Every growing format eventually attracts the same mistake: volume. Once
businesses hear that newsletters are valuable, the temptation is to launch one
because newsletters are valuable. A newsletter without an editorial reason to
exist becomes another recurring obligation competing for attention.

The question worth answering is what someone would consistently want from you
that they cannot get from an occasional social post. Analysis, maybe. Curation,
research, industry intelligence, original reporting, practical instruction, a
distinctive point of view, a community.

But there has to be an answer. Without one, the format is not a strategy. It is
a channel with a schedule.

## What I'm watching

If short-form production keeps getting easier and cheaper, there will be more
content competing for the same attention — and sustained thinking, research and
recognisable editorial judgment may become more valuable rather than less.

## The takeaway

Newsletters are not becoming interesting again because email suddenly became
new. They are becoming interesting because the rest of the internet changed
around them. When attention is fragmented, a recurring relationship matters.
When platforms control distribution, portability matters. When everyone can
publish, recognisable judgment matters.

Maybe the newsletter was never really the interesting part. Maybe the
interesting part is what it represents:

permission to continue the conversation.
