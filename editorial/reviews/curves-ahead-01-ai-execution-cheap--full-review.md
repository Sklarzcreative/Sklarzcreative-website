The draft's own note asked for exactly this: reduce generic AI-style rhetorical
repetition, "especially repeated short-paragraph theatrics and repeated 'X vs Y'
constructions," while preserving the thesis and voice. That note was right about
what is wrong with the piece, and the findings below mostly quantify it.

---

## A · Editorial verdict

### `NEEDS SUBSTANTIVE REVISION`

This is not a thesis problem. The argument is real, the framing is genuinely
analytic, and several lines are better than most published writing on the
subject. The vocabulary is clean in a way that is rare — not one instance of
*leverage*, *unlock*, *transformative*, *game changer*, or *seamless* in 1,186
words, and the hedging is calibrated rather than evasive.

Two things hold it back, and both are structural.

**The form fights the argument.** The draft is 93 paragraphs averaging 12.8
words each. Fifteen separate passages are stacks of one-line fragments. That
cadence is the most recognizable signature of machine-written prose, and it is
doing nothing here that ordinary paragraphs would not do better.

**Three sections make one section's argument.** "More output is not the same as
more value," "Automation can make the wrong process faster," and "The businesses
that benefit most may not be the ones using the most AI" all argue that applying
AI to an unclear thing does not fix the unclear thing. Said once, well, it is
the piece's best move. Said three times at rising levels of abstraction, it
reads as circling.

**Human voice, 5 → 8.** The lowest score on the draft, and the one most likely
to surprise you, because the stance is unmistakably yours. First person is
present and earned. The scepticism is real. The hedges are honest. What drags
it to 5 is purely rhythm: at 12.8 words per paragraph sustained across the whole
piece, the prose has one speed, and one speed reads as generated no matter whose
ideas are in it. The edit runs 30.5 words per paragraph with real variation
around it.

**Authority, 6 → 6. Unchanged, and this is the finding that matters most.**
There is not one concrete instance anywhere in the draft — no case, no named
situation, no moment from your own work. Authority in an argument like this comes
from evidence of having done the thing, and an editor cannot supply it without
fabricating it. Flagged in the text, not invented. This is the ceiling on the
piece and it stays until you add material only you have.

**Credibility, 8 → 8.** Already strong, deliberately left alone. The draft makes
no statistical or factual claim requiring a source, and every forecast is framed
as one. Nothing needed flagging under §6, which is unusual for a piece about a
fast-moving subject and worth saying out loud.

## C · Key editorial notes

**1 · Fifteen stacked lists in 1,186 words.**
Counting only passages of three or more one-line fragments: the friction list,
the produce-more list, the never-answered questions, the positioning-problem
list, the sometimes-AI triad, the three historical advantages, the
what-deserves-attention list of eight, the four artifacts, the six *why* 
questions, the not-necessarily list, the competent-content list, the
nine noticeable qualities, the five trust questions, the seven redesign
questions, and the four closing questions.

Any two or three of these would be effective. Fifteen makes it the article's
default way of thinking rather than a device, which is precisely §4.6. The edit
keeps two — the *sometimes AI is the answer* triad and the closing
*execution/judgment* pair — and converts the rest to prose. Almost nothing was
cut; it was rejoined.

**2 · Eight instances of the reframe-as-question device.**
"That is usually described as a productivity story / I think there is another
story underneath it." · "Companies are asking: Where can we add AI? / I think
the more useful question is…" · "I don't think the competitive question will
ultimately be… / A more interesting question is…" · plus five more.

§4.3 allows roughly one staged instance. The edit keeps one — the pivot into the
thesis in the opening, where the contrast is the actual point — and folds the
others into single sentences. The *where can we add AI / where is the work
breaking down* pair survives as one sentence rather than a staged
question-and-answer, which keeps the idea and loses the theatre.

**3 · Your sharpest sentence is 30% in, and it should be in the opening.**
"It makes avoiding them more expensive because unclear thinking can now be
distributed at extraordinary speed."

That is the thesis in its most compelling form, and it arrives after five
one-sentence paragraphs of ramp that end on "And that distinction is going to
matter" — a promise to say something rather than the thing itself. The edit
promotes the line, and the concrete images that follow it ("a positioning
problem can become thirty posts"), into the first three paragraphs. Your actual
opening line — "There has never been a cheaper time to make something look
finished" — is good and is untouched. *Look finished* is doing real work.

**4 · Your best two sentences are in section nine of ten.**
"Technology tends to make capability visible. Strategy often looks like choosing
not to use all of it."

That is the most quotable thing in the draft and the most original, and it is
buried as the third item in a watching-list near the end. The edit leaves it in
"What I'm watching" but restructures the section so it lands as the closing
beat rather than a bullet. Consider whether it deserves more than that — it may
be the actual argument of Edition 06.

**5 · Three sections, one argument.**
Merged. "More output is not the same as more value" now absorbs the automation
material and the organisational-redesign material, because all three were
demonstrating the same claim. The merge is where most of the 1,186 → 916 word
reduction came from, and no idea was dropped in it — check the *what should
remain human / automated / eliminated* material, which now reads as one clause
instead of a seven-line stack.

**6 · No example anywhere. Flagged, not invented.**
See the first bracketed note in the edited text. Your own backlog suggests you
have the material — *What I Learned Trying to Build a Low-Cost Marketing Stack*,
*The Tools That Stayed After I Tested Everything Else*, *Building Systems Before
You Have a Team*. One paragraph about a tool you added and later removed would
move this from argued to demonstrated. It is the largest single gain available
and I cannot make it for you.

**7 · The obvious objection goes unanswered.**
A sceptical reader will think: these systems are getting better at
judgment-shaped work too, and "judgment is the durable advantage" is a
convenient belief for a strategist to hold. Your voice is comfortable
challenging your own assumptions; the piece would be much harder to dismiss for
conceding this on your own terms. Flagged in the text. I have not written the
concession, because your answer is yours and I do not know it.

**8 · A trade the edit makes, so you can reverse it.**
The conclusion's four-question stack — *What matters? What is worth doing? What
should we ignore? And what should happen next?* — was cut. It is the third
appearance of that question set in the piece, and by then it restates rather
than lands. But it also gave the ending a slow, deliberate wind-down before
"Execution is getting cheaper. Judgment isn't." The edited close is faster and
harder. If it now feels abrupt, that cut is why, and putting one of those four
questions back would fix it without reinstating the stack.

## E · Change summary

- **Promoted the thesis.** Moved "unclear thinking can now be distributed at
  extraordinary speed" and its concrete examples into the opening; cut the
  five-paragraph ramp and the "that distinction is going to matter" placeholder.
  Your first line is unchanged.
- **Rejoined thirteen of fifteen fragment stacks into prose.** Paragraph length
  went from 12.8 words to 30.5, with real variation. This is the bulk of the
  human-voice and AI-slop movement.
- **Merged three overlapping sections into one**, preserving every distinct idea
  inside them, including the organisational-redesign questions.
- **Reduced the reframe-as-question device from eight instances to one.**
- **Restructured "What I'm watching"** so the capability/restraint line lands as
  the section's payoff instead of a list item.
- **Tightened the conclusion** and cut its restated question stack, keeping
  "Execution is getting cheaper. Judgment isn't."
- **1,186 → 916 words**, with nothing removed from the argument.
- **Flagged two things rather than fixing them**: the absence of any concrete
  example, and the unanswered objection. Both need you.

### Not changed

No claim was strengthened, softened, or added. No statistic, source, anecdote,
client story or experience was introduced — and the draft's calibrated hedging
("I think," "I suspect," "may," "increasingly") was preserved throughout rather
than sharpened into false confidence. The thesis, the order of the argument, and
every position in it are as they were. Every sentence in the edited version is
yours verbatim, yours compressed, or joining between two of yours.
