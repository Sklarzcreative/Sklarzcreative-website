There has never been a cheaper time to make something look finished.

A business can produce a presentation, campaign concept, research summary,
video, landing page, content calendar or month's worth of social posts in a
fraction of the time those things once required.

That is usually described as a productivity story.

I think there is another story underneath it.

When execution becomes abundant, deciding what deserves to be executed becomes
more important.

AI can help businesses produce more. It does not automatically make what they
produce worth producing.

And that distinction is going to matter.

## We spent years treating execution as the bottleneck

A good idea used to encounter friction almost immediately.

Someone had to research it.
Someone had to write it.
Someone had to design it.
Someone had to edit it.
Someone had to resize it, format it, schedule it, distribute it and measure it.

Even relatively simple marketing work consumed meaningful amounts of time and
money.

That friction naturally limited output.

Now much of it is disappearing.

The same team can explore more concepts, create more variations and move from
idea to execution much faster.

That is valuable.

But removing a bottleneck does not guarantee that what flows through it is good.

Sometimes it simply exposes the next bottleneck.

And increasingly, I think that bottleneck is judgment.

## More output is not the same as more value

Consider a business that is struggling with content.

The obvious response is increasingly easy:

Produce more posts.
Generate more graphics.
Make more videos.
Repurpose everything.
Automate distribution.

The machinery can now do all of that remarkably well.

But suppose the real problem is that the company has never clearly answered:

Why should someone choose us?
Who are we actually for?
What do we understand that our competitors don't?
What should people associate with our name?
What are we trying to make happen with this piece of communication?

AI does not make those questions disappear.

It makes avoiding them more expensive because unclear thinking can now be
distributed at extraordinary speed.

A positioning problem can become 30 posts.
A weak idea can become a campaign.
An unnecessary workflow can become an automation.
A confused strategy can suddenly look extremely polished.

## Automation can make the wrong process faster

I've become increasingly skeptical of adding tools simply because they can
perform a task.

The better question is:

What job is this tool replacing or improving?

If the answer isn't clear, adding technology can create another workflow instead
of simplifying one.

There is a version of this happening across businesses right now.

Companies are asking:

Where can we add AI?

I think the more useful question is:

Where is the work actually breaking down?

Sometimes AI is the answer.
Sometimes the process should be redesigned first.
Sometimes the task should disappear altogether.

The ability to automate something is not evidence that it should exist.

## The scarce resource is moving upstream

When production was expensive, production capability itself created advantage.
When information was difficult to access, access to information created
advantage.
When distribution was limited, distribution created advantage.

As those things become increasingly accessible, advantage moves somewhere else.

Toward questions like:

What deserves attention?
Which information is credible?
Which opportunity matters?
Which audience matters?
Which idea is differentiated?
Which signal is meaningful?
What should we ignore?
What should happen next?

Those are judgment questions.

They are also strategy questions.

And they become more important, not less, when the cost of acting on an answer
approaches zero.

## This changes what expertise looks like

Expertise has often been demonstrated through production.

The strategist produced the strategy deck.
The researcher produced the report.
The writer produced the article.
The designer produced the visual system.

Those artifacts made expertise visible.

But if increasingly capable tools can help produce the artifact, then the
artifact alone becomes weaker evidence of expertise.

The value moves toward the thinking behind it.

Why this recommendation instead of the other ten?
Why this positioning?
Why this evidence?
Why this audience?
Why this message?
Why now?

Good work will increasingly need to demonstrate not merely that something was
produced, but that someone made intelligent choices about what to produce and
why.

## Content is an obvious example

The internet is about to contain an extraordinary amount of competent content.

Not necessarily excellent content.
Not necessarily original content.
Not necessarily useful content.
But competent content.

Clean graphics.
Readable articles.
Acceptable videos.
Reasonable captions.
Perfectly serviceable explainers.

When competence becomes abundant, competence stops being much of a
differentiator.

What becomes more noticeable?

A distinctive point of view.
Original research.
Experience.
Taste.
Credibility.
Specificity.
Evidence.
Unexpected connections.
A willingness to say that something popular may not actually matter.

In other words, the things that require judgment.

## This is also why trust becomes more important

Abundance creates another problem: verification.

If producing information becomes nearly effortless, people have more information
to evaluate and fewer reasons to assume that any individual piece deserves their
attention.

That makes trust more valuable.

Who produced this?
What do they know?
What evidence are they using?
Do they have a reason to exaggerate?
Have they been consistently useful before?

The next phase of digital competition may therefore be less about who can
produce the most and more about who becomes worth listening to.

That is a very different objective.

## The businesses that benefit most may not be the ones using the most AI

I don't think the competitive question will ultimately be:

Who adopted AI fastest?

AI capabilities are spreading too quickly for access alone to remain a durable
advantage.

A more interesting question is:

Who redesigned their organization around what those capabilities make possible?

That means deciding:

What should remain human?
What should be automated?
What should be eliminated?
Where does expertise matter most?
Where does speed actually create value?
Where does additional output simply create noise?
And where does technology allow a small organization to behave like a much
larger one without inheriting the larger organization's complexity?

Those are strategic decisions.

The tools matter.

The architecture around the tools matters more.

## What I'm watching

I'm particularly interested in three shifts.

First, the premium on taste.

When almost anyone can generate ten plausible options, choosing the right one
becomes more valuable.

Second, the premium on trust.

As synthetic and derivative material becomes easier to produce, identifiable
people, credible expertise and evidence become stronger signals.

Third, the premium on restraint.

The ability to produce everything may make knowing what not to produce a
competitive advantage.

That last one may be the hardest.

Technology tends to make capability visible.

Strategy often looks like choosing not to use all of it.

## The takeaway

AI is reducing the cost of execution.

That is significant.

But cheaper execution does not eliminate strategy. It increases the consequences
of weak strategy because almost any decision can now be turned into output
quickly.

The businesses, creators and professionals who benefit most won't necessarily be
the ones who produce the most.

They'll be the ones who become better at deciding:

What matters?
What is worth doing?
What should we ignore?
And what should happen next?

Execution is getting cheaper.

Judgment isn't.
