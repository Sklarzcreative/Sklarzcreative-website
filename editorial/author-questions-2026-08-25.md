## Edition 01 - *AI Is Making Execution Cheap. Judgment Is Becoming More Valuable.*

### Q1 · One concrete example. A tool you added and later removed.

**Why it's needed.** There is not one concrete instance anywhere in the piece —
no case, no scenario, no moment from your own work. Authority scored 6 before
the edit and 6 after, because that ceiling is not an editor's to lift. Your own
backlog suggests you have the material: *What I Learned Trying to Build a
Low-Cost Marketing Stack*, *The Tools That Stayed After I Tested Everything
Else*, *Building Systems Before You Have a Team*.

**Where it goes.** The automation section, right after *"The ability to automate
something is not evidence that it should exist."*

**What I need:**

- What was the tool, and what job did you add it to do?
- What actually happened — did it replace work, or add a layer?
- Why did you drop it (or keep it)?
- Anything you'd rather I didn't name in public — a client, a price, a vendor?

Two or three sentences of raw notes is plenty. If nothing fits, say so and I'll
cut the flagged note rather than leave a gap where an example should be.

**Approved answer:**

> I set up an automated tracker for Curves Ahead because I wanted the publishing
> work and progress centralized. Instead of replacing a clear workflow, it
> became another system to monitor while higher-priority client work needed
> attention. I turned it off until it had a defined job and a real workflow to
> replace.

---

### Q2 · Your answer to the obvious objection.

**Why it's needed.** A sceptical reader will already have thought it, and the
piece never engages it — which makes it easier to dismiss than it deserves.

**The objection, stated as harshly as a reader would:**

> These systems are getting better at judgment-shaped work too — evaluating,
> ranking, critiquing, choosing between options. And "judgment is the durable
> advantage" is a very convenient thing for a strategist to believe about the
> future of strategists.

**What I need:** what you actually think. Any of these is a real answer:

- The objection is wrong, and here's why —
- It's partly right, and the thesis narrows to —
- It's right and I don't have an answer yet, but I still think —

**The third option is a legitimate answer** and often the strongest one. Your
voice is comfortable saying "I don't know yet"; the piece is weaker for
pretending the question isn't there.

**Approved answer:**

> The objection is partly right. AI will increasingly perform work that looks
> like judgment, including ranking, critiquing, and choosing among options. The
> durable human advantage is not making every choice manually. It is defining
> the goal, deciding which tradeoffs matter, evaluating context the system may
> not hold, and remaining accountable for the consequences.

## Edition 03 - *Why Newsletters Are Becoming Interesting Again*

### Q3 · One concrete example. Something you've actually observed.

**Why it's needed.** Same gap, same ceiling. Edition 03 has an advantage

newsletters — but I can't use that unless the observation is real.

**Where it goes.** Best placement is the community-loop section, or "newsletters

**What I need — any one of these:**

- A newsletter you subscribe to, and what specifically keeps you subscribed
- Something you noticed testing across platforms — where a post got discovery
  but no relationship, or the reverse

Notes, not prose.

**Approved answer:**

> Testing across multiple social platforms showed me that visibility and
> relationship are not the same thing. A post can receive discovery or
> engagement and then disappear without creating an ongoing connection. A
> newsletter gives the ideas a continuing home and gives readers a deliberate
> way to return.

### Q4 · Which of these two open questions you actually hold.

**Why it's needed.** Two of your three "what I'm watching" items restated
sections the reader had just finished, so I cut them. The section is now thin,
and I'd rather it were genuinely forward-looking than padded back out.

I drafted two candidates as *notes only* — they are objections to your argument,
not positions you have taken, and putting them in your voice would invent a view
you haven't expressed:

**(a) Whether inbox attention holds.** The whole argument rests on subscription
attention being scarce. The incentive to launch a newsletter is now widely
understood — if everyone arrives, the scarcity erodes. Where's the ceiling?

**(b) Whether "owned" survives the details.** An email list is more portable
than a follower count, but deliverability, spam filtering and open rates are
gatekept too — just by different parties, less visibly. Portable is not the same
as unmediated.

**What I need:** **a**, **b**, both, neither, or your own third thing. If you
pick one, one line on what you actually think about it is enough.

**Approved answer:**

> Both questions matter. Inbox attention can become crowded, and an email list
> is portable rather than completely owned because deliverability and filtering
> are still mediated. Newsletters remain valuable because the relationship is
> more direct, but only when the content earns attention rather than treating
> the inbox as guaranteed reach.
