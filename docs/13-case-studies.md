## Why `/work/` says what it currently says

The page reads:

> Detailed case studies are being written up. In the meantime, this is an honest
> account of the practice areas, how engagements are structured, the method
> underneath them, and the work that is already published.

That was the right call for launch. The alternative was inventing proof, and on
a site whose entire argument is that credibility must be checkable, fabricated
case studies would not just be dishonest — they would be self-refuting.

But it is a placeholder, and it reads as one. Three real case studies would do
more for enquiry quality than anything else left on the list, because they are
the only thing on the site that demonstrates judgement rather than describing it.

---

## What clears first

Before anything else: **what are you allowed to publish?**

For each engagement, one of three:

1. **Named** — client name, logo, specifics. Strongest by a wide margin.
2. **Described** — *a science communications organisation*, *a documentary
   production*. Nearly as strong if the sector and scale are concrete.
3. **Nothing** — not usable, skip it.

If you are unsure, ask. A short email — *I'd like to write up our work together
for my site, here's the draft, are you comfortable with it?* — converts a
category 2 into a category 1 surprisingly often, and people say yes more readily
when they see the draft first. It is worth sending before I write, not after.

## The intake — answer these per engagement

Fragments. Not prose.

**If you would rather be interviewed than fill in a form**, there is a prompt
that does exactly that: [`handoff/chatgpt-case-study-interview.md`](../handoff/chatgpt-case-study-interview.md).
Paste it into ChatGPT, answer its questions, and send me the structured block it
produces at the end. Same material, less staring at a blank page.

**1. The situation you walked into**
What was actually wrong? Not the brief you were given — the underlying problem.
*"Four workstreams, three agencies, no one owning the publishing calendar."*

**2. Context that made it hard**
Constraints, politics, timeline, technical limits, a regulated sector, a
sceptical internal stakeholder. This is the part that makes a case study read as
real rather than as a portfolio caption.

**3. Your actual role**
Where did your responsibility begin and end? Who else was involved and doing
what? Precision here is credibility — *"strategy and editorial direction; the
studio handled production"* is stronger than *"led the project"*.

**4. The strategic decision**
The one call that changed the shape of the work. What did you decide, what was
the alternative, and why did you choose as you did?

**This is the most important answer on the page.** It is the only one that shows
how you think, and it is what a prospective client is actually shopping for.
Anyone can list deliverables.

**5. What got produced**
Concrete outputs. *"Editorial framework, launch plan, twelve-episode structure,
brand guidelines, four campaign templates."*

**6. The outcome, or what was learned**
Whatever you can honestly say. **No vanity metrics, and no need to reach for
one.** All of these are legitimate:

- *"Launched on schedule, which had not happened on the previous two attempts."*
- *"Reduced from four review cycles to one."*
- *"The framework is still in use two years later."*
- *"The audience research changed the positioning before launch rather than
  after."*
- *"Halfway through it became clear the real problem was ownership, not
  messaging. Naming that was the useful part."*

That last kind — an honest learning, including one that redirected the
engagement — is more persuasive to a serious buyer than a percentage they cannot
verify. Percentages you cannot source read as decoration, and this audience
discounts them automatically.

**7. One sentence a client said**, if you have one and can quote it. Optional,
and a real fragment beats a polished testimonial.

## The structure I will write them into

| Section | Length | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title | one line | The problem, not the client name |
| Sector · Role · Year | one line | Scannable context |
| **The situation** | 2–3 sentences | What was wrong |
| **The complication** | 2–3 sentences | Why it was not simple |
| **The strategic decision** | 1 short paragraph | The call, and the alternative |
| **The work** | a list | Deliverables |
| **What happened** | 2–3 sentences | Outcome or learning, unembellished |
| Close | one line | Link to the relevant capability |

Roughly 300–400 words each. Long enough to demonstrate thinking, short enough to
be read. Three of these on `/work/` is the target — enough to show range, few
enough that each one is clearly deliberate.

### What that shape produces

Filled in, a case study reads like this — with **every bracket a placeholder for
your material, not a claim about work that happened:**

> ### Four workstreams, one calendar
> [Sector] · Strategy and editorial direction · [Year]
>
> **The situation.** [What was actually wrong, in two sentences.]
>
> **The complication.** [The constraint that made the obvious answer unavailable.]
>
> **The strategic decision.** [The call you made, the alternative you rejected,
> and why. This is the paragraph the case study exists for.]
>
> **The work.** [Deliverables, as a list.]
>
> **What happened.** [The honest outcome. A learning counts.]

## What I do once you send it

1. Draft all three, in the site's voice.
2. Send them back for your approval — and for the client's, where a name is used.
3. Build them as three pages under `/work/`, on the existing design system,
   with `Article` structured data and their own OG cards.
4. Replace the placeholder sentence in the `/work/` page hero, add a case-study
   grid above the practice areas, and cross-link each study to the capability it
   demonstrates.
5. Add them to `sitemap.xml` and request indexing.

**Two engagements is enough to start.** Three is better, but two published beats
three in a drawer, and the third can land later without any rework.
